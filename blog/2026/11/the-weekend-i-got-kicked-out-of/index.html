The Weekend I Got Kicked Out of a Preschool (and Kept Building TeachersDeck Anyway)

17 November 2026 · 6 min read

Ho Chi Minh City, 14–16 November 2026

For the last few weeks I've been living in a preschool. Not the most glamorous office for a startup founder, but it was a roof, a corner to work in, and it let me keep building TeachersDeck while I saved up for a place of my own. This weekend that arrangement ended, and not quietly.

The week before: an expensive visa run

It helps to know how the week started. On Sunday 8 November I rode out to the Mộc Bài border crossing to do a visa run into Cambodia and back. It took longer and cost far more than I'd planned. Between the trip, the fees and the work I missed, that week cost me about 5 million VND. That's money I'd been saving for a room of my own, and it's why I was still living at the preschool.




Friday: "Work during the day like everyone else"

Anyone who follows this blog knows how I work. In the afternoons I teach English, math and science at a public school. TeachersDeck gets built at night: coding, recording English audio, answering messages, usually until about 6am. Then I sleep for a few hours in the morning before heading to school.

On Friday afternoon the school owner told me that wasn't going to work anymore. Her messages, translated by Zalo, said it plainly: she couldn't let me sleep during the day, I could only sleep at night "when we all go to bed," and she didn't "benefit from" my night work or have "any responsibility" for my job. 

I asked why I couldn't keep the upstairs room I'd been working in. Another teacher had said it was fine. The owner said that teacher "does not have full authority," that the floor was being rented out, and to "stop asking why." When I asked about renting a small space myself, she quoted a 4 million VND deposit plus 2–4 million a month. We went back and forth about whether the law sets the deposit (it doesn't, really; it's up to the two parties, and she sent me a screenshot saying so). Her offer was to take me at 6:40 the next morning to another campus where I could stay during the day.

That would have meant working downstairs in a room with a class going on, taking phone calls and recording audio in front of preschoolers. I said so. The reply was: "I'm the owner here, and I have the right to decide everything… I am not responsible for your life."

At 10:47pm the messages started again. I hadn't returned the bed I was using, and she would "call the police tomorrow morning to expel you immediately." She also reminded me that she can see the rooms through the school cameras. Those are the same rooms where I've been showering and changing.

Saturday: the police

I hadn't slept in two days. At 6:39am I wrote "Good night, please do not disturb my sleep." Her answer was to leave her house "right away today." By 9am she told me she'd called and the police were on the way. Honestly, at that point I'd already told her to go ahead and call them. All I wanted was to sleep.

The rest of Saturday played out over text messages with the police officer handling it. Here's the short version:

The hotel. The owner agreed to pay for a week at a hotel, but only the one she picked, across the bridge from the school where I actually work. I'd already found one near my work, only about 100k a day more, and waited there two hours for someone to book it. The officer's answer: "We picked that hotel, accept it."

Getting there. I said I had no money for gas. He said I could push my motorbike the 600 meters.

My passport. My belongings had been packed while I was out. When I asked where my passport was, the officer said he'd put it in my backpack himself: front pouch, then right-side zipper, then maybe the back. "Don't be lazy," he wrote, and "you will find it eventually." I couldn't find it. A green army bag of mine was missing too, and there was a black bag in another classroom that wasn't mine.

The ultimatum. "Last chance, go there now and she will pay for one week, otherwise, we're done. Yes or no?" I went.

The hotel was full. When I got there nobody had heard of the booking, and then they told me they were fully booked. I messaged the owner to book the other hotel instead. The hotel told her it was full too. She blocked me on Zalo, and then on every account.

So I spent Saturday night where a lot of people in this city end up when they've got nowhere else to go: at a GS25 convenience store. I had a laptop, a power outlet, a cold drink and the whole night ahead of me, so I did what I always do at night. I worked on TeachersDeck until morning.

Sunday

Sunday morning I still had no room. The owner had blocked me everywhere, and I still didn't have all my things. I texted the officer one more time: if she won't book the hotel, have her send the money for the week, 3,040,000 VND, straight to my account, and I'll find my own room.

Some good news: I found my passport. The rest of my things are still at the preschool, and the owner has kept them.

Sunday night was a repeat of Saturday, just under a different logo. I spent it at a 7-Eleven, plugged in and working on TeachersDeck until the sun came up.

Monday: a room, finally

On Monday morning, before I went to teach, 2 million VND I'd borrowed landed in my account. I thought finding a room would be the easy part after that. It wasn't, and this time the problem wasn't money. There was a paperwork issue with registering me as a guest, so hotels wouldn't check me in, even with cash in my hand and a valid passport. I'm still sorting that out.

In the end a backpackers hostel took me, at 140k a night. It's a bunk, a locker and a shower I can use without wondering who's watching on a camera. After the weekend I'd had, it felt like a palace.

What I'm taking from this

I'm grateful I had a place to stay at all, and I said so at the time. But being grateful for a favour doesn't mean you can live by someone else's clock when your work runs at night. Being told to "work during the day like everyone else" is the line I'll remember. Plenty of people building something on their own time can't do that.

I'm a foreigner here, and this weekend showed me how fast "helping you out" can turn into having no say at all, with your bed, your bags and your passport in someone else's hands. Next time I'll get it in writing: a real rental, a real receipt, my own key. I'm also keeping a closer eye on my paperwork, because one bad week of it cost me my savings right when I needed them most.

TeachersDeck didn't stop. The apps are live, teachers are using them, and I'll be back at it tonight, from wherever I'm sitting. If you're a teacher who's ever been told your hours don't count, I see you.

— Jerry
